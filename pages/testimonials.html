Participants and Alumni

Testimonials

APIGA India is a community-level initiative run for and with students and early-career professionals in India. The accounts below are written by participants and alumni about the programme and the work that followed it.

Pali Pranjal

APIGA India 2026

APIGA India was where my Internet governance journey really began. Before the program, I had only written a piece on domain name abuse out of curiosity and I did not really know there was an entire world of Internet governance around it. APIGA India introduced me to that world, as well as to a community of people approaching the Internet from very different vantage points. I built my foundations in IG through expert sessions and the Model ICANN simulation at APIGA India. It eventually led me to the regional APIGA in Busan, which was also my first international experience.

I am still very much at the beginning of this journey, so I do not have a formula for getting into Internet governance. But I would say: start somewhere. It could be writing about an issue you are curious about, taking free courses offered by ICANN, APNIC or ISOC, applying to Youth IGF or NetMission, getting involved with an Internet Society chapter, submitting ICANN public comments, or just showing up to an open public meeting.

Anika Vashishth

APIGA India 2025

Internet governance is the web of technical bodies, policymakers, civil society and businesses who decide, together, how the internet stays open, secure and available to everyone. Most people never see this layer, but it shapes who gets access and how safe that access is.

APIGA India opened me up to these nuances. I have spent years in compliance, but I had never understood the internet at a root level (pun very much intended). It introduced me to the organisations working tirelessly, and mostly invisibly, to keep the internet open and accessible, and as a cybersecurity professional, it helped me trace risk further upstream, to the governance issues that ripple out to people, infrastructure and entire countries.

Being part of APIGA matters because these decisions do not stay abstract. They become the access people have and the risks they are exposed to, and being at that table means having a say in an internet that is still being shaped.

As an APIGA India alumna, I got to take that further and see internet governance at work in Korea, watching these discussions play out in the room rather than reading about them from a distance. It confirmed why this kind of exposure matters so early in a career.

You do not need a technical or policy background to belong here. I came in from compliance, and all it asked of me was curiosity.

To anyone early in their career and curious about this space: if a chance like this comes your way, take it.

Chirag Singh

APIGA India 2025

APIGA was not only something I discovered later in my life, but was actually a life-changing opportunity that helped me navigate through India's Internet ecosystem and understand its governance. It helped me break down how smaller components such as TLDs (Top-Level Domains), domain names and networks work, how different parts of the Internet infrastructure function, how data moves across networks and jurisdictions, and how important the multistakeholder model actually is. I came to understand that the Internet is not governed by a single stakeholder, but through the participation of governments, the private sector, technical communities, civil society, academia and, importantly, youth. Youth not only holds a bigger role to play, but their opinions and participation can actually help shape the future of the Internet.

I also began to understand how data is transferred through secure networks from one place to another, how phishing and cyberattacks can affect users, how cyberbullying needs to be tackled, and why cybersecurity and data protection are becoming increasingly important.

India, being one of the largest populations in the world and an increasingly important centre for technological advancement, is viewed by many countries as an important stakeholder in the development of the global digital ecosystem. As India continues to develop and change its approach to technology and Internet Governance, I began to understand how local networks and digital policies in India can have a much wider influence and how data is governed, protected and transferred across the world.

Professionally and personally, I grew in an amazing way through this experience. I started teaching and imparting awareness to young people, not only in my college but also to students graduating from school, about how AI and technology play a bigger role in our lives and how we, as users and future Internet guardians, have a responsibility to protect and shape the digital environment around us. I conducted workshops and attended several conferences where I spoke about my journey and what APIGA taught me. I also participated in debates and discussions about what changes Internet Governance in India should include and explored possible solutions to emerging challenges.

APIGA made me realise that Internet Governance is not merely a technical subject; it connects technology with policy, rights, security, development, inclusion and the everyday lives of people. Just like our environment, the Internet and our data are also important resources that need to be protected and responsibly managed at this stage.

Young people should not remain passive users of the Internet. We have a voice, a responsibility and an opportunity to participate in the conversations that will determine what the Internet looks like for generations to come.
